Chapter 10 · Learned Images and Learned Scenes

CSS 551 · Advanced 3D Computer Graphics — course text
Primary reading for Week 10. Pairs with the L10 lecture (“PBR, Diffusion Models & Learned Scenes”) and the Final Project studio.

Nine weeks of this course built a renderer from first principles: a scene is data, three matrices carry a vertex to the screen, a surface is a mesh dressed by a texture and lit by a local model. This chapter is about the two things that renderer cannot do, and about the machinery that does them. It cannot balance the light energy at a point, so we finish the illumination story with the rendering equation. And it cannot draw a picture from nothing: it needs a scene. The second half of the chapter is about models that learn, either an image-generating function from an archive of photographs (diffusion models) or one scene from its own photographs (neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting). The lecture keeps the mathematics to one line at a time and shows everything on live exhibits; this chapter is where the lines are written out, including a treatment of images as functions that the lecture only mentions in passing.

1 · Honest light: the rendering equation and PBR

Session 9 lit a point from the lights, the normal, and the eye, and nothing else in the scene; the ambient constant stood in for all the light that bounces. Three things follow that no patch can fix: the model can reflect more light than arrives, it does not promise that swapping the light and the eye leaves the picture unchanged, and it has no indirect light at all. They are one gap: the local model never balances the light energy at a point. The equation that does was written down by Kajiya in 1986 (Chapter 1, Section 3). With \(\omega_o\) the direction toward the eye, \(\omega_i\) a direction light arrives from, and \(\mathbf{n}\) the surface normal,

\[ L_o(\mathbf{x}, \omega_o) \;=\; L_e(\mathbf{x}, \omega_o) \;+\; \int_{\Omega} f_r(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i, \omega_o)\, L_i(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i)\, (\mathbf{n}\cdot\omega_i)\, d\omega_i . \]

Read it in words: the radiance leaving a point toward the eye is what the point emits, plus the sum over every incoming direction of the arriving radiance, weighted by the material’s answer \(f_r\) (the bidirectional reflectance distribution function, BRDF) and by the cosine of the arrival angle. It is hard because \(L_i\) on the right is some other point’s \(L_o\): light bounces, and the equation refers to itself. Path tracing estimates the integral by following random paths; real-time engines approximate it with a local term plus tricks.

The material term is where physically based rendering (PBR) lives. A rough surface is modeled as a field of microscopic mirrors (microfacets); the Cook–Torrance specular BRDF is a product of a normal-distribution term \(D\) (what fraction of facets face the half-vector; roughness lives here, and GGX is the modern default shape), a shadowing-masking term \(G\), and a Fresnel term \(F\), over a normalization:

\[ f_r^{\text{spec}} \;=\; \frac{D(\mathbf{h})\, G(\omega_i,\omega_o)\, F(\omega_i,\mathbf{h})} {4\, (\mathbf{n}\cdot\omega_i)(\mathbf{n}\cdot\omega_o)} . \]

On top of it, every engine exposes two artist sliders: metallic (a dielectric shows a colored diffuse term under a weak white specular; a metal has no diffuse term and a specular tinted by its base color) and roughness (Unity’s smoothness is \(1 - \) roughness). Those are the sliders in the Standard Shader, and the same parameterization is what glTF and every modern renderer exchange.

See it live BRDF lobes: the specular lobe of the course’s Cornell scene as roughness changes, Phong beside a GGX-shaped distribution. The two mappings from roughness to lobe width are not calibrated to each other; the demo shows shape, not units.

2 · A network is a function

Definition (neural network) A neural network is a function \(f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}\) built from alternating linear maps and fixed nonlinearities, whose behavior is set by a vector of weights \(\theta\). The two-layer perceptron of the lecture’s exhibit A is \[ f_\theta(x) \;=\; b_2 + \sum_{h=1}^{H} w^{(2)}_h \,\tanh\!\big(w^{(1)}_h x + b^{(1)}_h\big), \qquad \theta = (w^{(1)}, b^{(1)}, w^{(2)}, b_2) \in \mathbb{R}^{3H+1}. \]

Training is not programming. Given examples \((x_i, y_i)\), define a loss such as the mean squared error \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (f_\theta(x_i) - y_i)^2\), and repeat: compute the gradient \(\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\) (by the chain rule, organized as backpropagation, Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams 1986), and move every weight a little against it, \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\). Two facts carry the rest of the chapter. First, capacity: a network with enough hidden units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain to any accuracy (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991). Second, and more important for what follows, between the examples a trained network is smooth: it interpolates rather than snapping to the nearest data point. A lookup table has the first property trivially and the second not at all.

See it live A network that learns: the 37-weight perceptron above is trained in your browser by gradient descent on twelve points of a hidden curve. Drag epochs from 0 and watch the curve bend a few steps per frame while the loss plot falls; try 2 hidden units (it cannot bend enough) and 40 (it fits a step with a smooth ramp).
Worked example With \(H = 12\) the perceptron has \(3\cdot 12 + 1 = 37\) weights. Full-batch gradient descent on twelve points at learning rate \(0.05\) takes the error from about \(1.7\) (random weights) to \(8\times10^{-3}\) after 400 steps and \(8\times10^{-4}\) after 3000; you can read those numbers off the demo. Each step costs one forward and one backward pass over twelve points, a few thousand multiplications, which is why it runs at frame rate. The million-weight network of Section 6 trains by the same loop on sixty thousand images; only the sizes change.

3 · Embeddings, encoders, decoders, and one shared space

Definition (embedding) An embedding of a set of things (words, digits, images) is a map from each thing to a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), learned so that things which behave alike land near each other. Similarity is measured by distance or, more often, by the cosine of the angle between vectors, \(\cos(u, v) = \frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}\).

Nobody places the points. A network puts them where its task goes better; the famous 2013 result was that word vectors trained only to predict neighboring words end up supporting arithmetic, with “king” minus “man” plus “woman” landing near “queen” (Mikolov et al.). The lecture’s exhibit B is a smaller and more honest version: the digit-drawing network of Section 6 carries a table of eleven 64-dimensional class vectors it learned only from denoising, and digits that share strokes ended up as neighbors (4 nearest 9 with cosine \(0.54\), 3 nearest 5 with \(0.52\), 7 nearest 9 with \(0.48\)).

See it live Embeddings, read from the weights: the class table of the trained denoiser projected to its two principal axes (which keep about a third of the variance; the readout’s cosines use all 64 dimensions).

An encoder is a network that maps a thing to its embedding, \(E : \text{thing} \to \mathbb{R}^d\); a decoder maps back, \(D : \mathbb{R}^d \to \text{thing}\). Trained together to reproduce their input through a narrow middle, \(\min_{E,D} \sum_i \|D(E(x_i)) - x_i\|^2\), they form an autoencoder, and the middle is forced to keep only what matters (Hinton & Salakhutdinov 2006). Stable Diffusion’s autoencoder is exactly this, with a variational twist that keeps the latent well behaved: a \(512\times512\times3\) image becomes a \(64\times64\times4\) latent, 48 times fewer numbers.

Two encoders, one space. Train an image encoder \(E_I\) and a text encoder \(E_T\) side by side on hundreds of millions of (image, caption) pairs with a contrastive objective: for each batch, a matching pair must have a higher cosine than every mismatched pair,

\[ \mathcal{L} \;=\; -\sum_{i} \log \frac{\exp\big(\cos(E_I(x_i), E_T(t_i))/\tau\big)} {\sum_{j} \exp\big(\cos(E_I(x_i), E_T(t_j))/\tau\big)} , \]

with a temperature \(\tau\). After training, a sentence and a photograph are vectors in the same space, so “aim the picture at this caption” becomes geometry. This is CLIP (Radford et al. 2021), and every concept you can say in words is now a coordinate.

4 · The space of images: vectors, functions, and the thin sheet

4.1 The finite-dimensional view

Definition (image as a vector) An \(m\times n\) RGB image is a point of the vector space \(\mathbb{R}^{3mn}\): list its numbers in a fixed order, \(\mathbf{x} = (r_{11}, g_{11}, b_{11}, r_{12}, \dots, b_{mn})\), and it is one point, or equivalently the vector from the origin to that point. Displayable images occupy the cube \([0,1]^{3mn}\). A \(128\times128\) RGB image has \(49{,}152\) coordinates; a \(512\times512\) one has \(786{,}432\).

Everything the pipeline does to images is now linear algebra on that vector. Averaging two images is adding two vectors and halving; a blur is a linear operator; the difference between two frames is a vector whose length is a measure of how much moved; the mean squared error between an image and its noisy version is \(\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|^2 / 3mn\), which is what the forward-process demo’s PSNR readout is built from. The picture to keep is that this space is enormous and almost entirely empty of meaning: draw a point uniformly at random from the cube and you get static, every time, because the set of images that look like anything is a thin, folded, low-dimensional sheet inside it. Generation is the problem of landing a new point on the sheet on purpose.

4.2 The function-space view

The vector view has a resolution baked in. A cleaner object is the image as a function: a rule that assigns a color to every point of the picture plane.

Definition (image as a function) A continuous image is a function \(f : [0,1]^2 \to [0,1]^3\), from the unit square to RGB. The set of all such functions with finite energy, \[ \mathcal{I} \;=\; \Big\{ f : [0,1]^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3 \;\Big|\; \int_{[0,1]^2} \|f(u,v)\|^2\, du\, dv < \infty \Big\}, \] is a vector space (the sum of two images and a scalar multiple of an image are images), with the inner product and norm \[ \langle f, g\rangle = \int_{[0,1]^2} f(u,v)\cdot g(u,v)\, du\, dv, \qquad \|f\| = \sqrt{\langle f, f\rangle}. \] It is infinite-dimensional: no finite list of numbers pins down an arbitrary \(f\).

The two views are related by sampling. An \(m\times n\) raster is the function evaluated on a grid, \(\mathbf{x}_{ij} = f(u_i, v_j)\) with \(u_i = (i - \tfrac12)/m\) and \(v_j = (j-\tfrac12)/n\); this is the “one look per pixel at its center” of Chapter 1’s rasterization slides. Going the other way, a raster defines a function by reconstruction: the nearest-neighbor rule gives a piecewise-constant \(f\) (a mosaic of \(3mn\) little box functions with the pixel values as coefficients), bilinear interpolation gives a continuous one, and a band-limited reconstruction gives the smoothest. In that language the vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3mn}\) is the list of coefficients of \(f\) in the box-function basis, and choosing a resolution is choosing how many basis functions to keep. Other bases exist and matter: the Fourier basis (sines and cosines of increasing frequency), in which JPEG stores images by keeping the low-frequency coefficients and discarding the rest, and wavelet bases, in which detail is organized by scale. An image is the same function in every basis; only the numbers change.

Aliasing, revisited Sampling is a linear map from \(\mathcal{I}\) to \(\mathbb{R}^{3mn}\) and it throws information away: two different functions can produce the same raster. When the discarded part matters (a checkerboard finer than the pixel grid, an edge crossing pixels at an angle) the samples tell a false story. That is aliasing, the disease of Chapter 1’s raster-machines era, and the sampling theorem says exactly when it cannot happen: a function with no frequencies above \(B\) is recovered perfectly from samples spaced closer than \(1/2B\). Supersampling, mipmaps, and motion blur are all ways of removing frequencies the grid cannot carry before sampling, which is why they work.

Why bother with the function view in a course that renders rasters? Three reasons that pay off within this chapter. First, several of the pipeline’s operations are stated most honestly on functions: anti-aliasing is filtering before sampling; a texture is a function on \([0,1]^2\) that the UV map pulls onto a surface (Session 8), and a mipmap is that function at several bandwidths. Second, a scene can be a function too: Section 8’s NeRF is a network standing in for \(F : \mathbb{R}^3\times S^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3\times\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), color and density at every point and viewing direction, with no mesh anywhere, and the same idea (a network as a coordinate-to-value function, an implicit or neural field representation) now represents images, shapes, and volumes at any resolution. Third, the generative models of Sections 6 and 7 are stated on vectors of a fixed size, but what they learn is resolution-agnostic in spirit: the sheet of natural images is a property of the functions, and a model trained at one raster size is approximating the same sheet a model at another size approximates. Diffusion in the continuous setting (on function space directly) is an active research direction; this course stays in \(\mathbb{R}^{3mn}\), where every statement below is literal.

Worked example (two images, one distance) Let \(f\) be uniform mid-gray, \(f(u,v) = (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), and \(g\) a vertical black-to-white ramp, \(g(u,v) = (u,u,u)\). Then \(\|f - g\|^2 = 3\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (\tfrac12 - u)^2\, du\, dv = 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{12} = \tfrac14\), so \(\|f-g\| = \tfrac12\). Sample both on an \(m\times n\) grid and the per-pixel mean squared error of the rasters converges to the same \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) as \(m\to\infty\): the finite-dimensional distance is the Riemann sum of the function-space one. Resolution changes the count of numbers, not the geometry.

4.3 The thin sheet, and what it is made of

Whatever the view, the images that mean something form a small region: a curved, low-dimensional set often called the manifold of natural images, though it is neither smooth nor a single piece. Two facts about it make generation possible. It is thin: nearly every direction away from a photograph leads immediately to something that is not a photograph, which is why random points are static. And it is structured: moving along it changes content in ways that have names (a cat turns its head, the light warms, the lens zooms), so the sheet has coordinates you can say in words, which is exactly what the encoder of Section 3 computes.

5 · The concept map is not invertible

Let \(E\) be the image encoder. It is many-to-one by design: thousands of cat photographs land on one “cat” embedding, because throwing away which cat, which pose, and which light is the whole point of a concept. So \(E\) has no inverse function. The honest inverse of a concept is a conditional distribution \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid c)\) over images, and a generator has to draw from it. A decoder that took only the concept would be forced to return one image per caption forever (which is what early text-to-image systems did); something must supply the missing choices. Diffusion supplies them as noise: every sample starts from a different random point \(\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), which contains more than enough arbitrary information, and the model spends its steps turning that noise into a specific image consistent with \(c\). Different noise, different cat; the caption steers, the noise decides. A one-to-many problem becomes a sequence of one-to-one steps, each of which a network can learn.

6 · Diffusion: destroy, learn to undo, sample

6.1 The forward process

Fix a schedule \(\bar\alpha(t)\) that falls from \(1\) at \(t=0\) to \(0\) at \(t=1\) (the course’s demos use the cosine schedule of Nichol & Dhariwal 2021). The forward process mixes a clean image with Gaussian noise in a proportion set by \(t\):

\[ \mathbf{x}_t \;=\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha(t)}\;\mathbf{x}_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha(t)}\;\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I). \]

Nothing is learned here. At \(t=1\) every image has become the same standard Gaussian, which is the whole point: that is the one distribution we can sample from trivially. The signal-to-noise ratio is \(\bar\alpha/(1-\bar\alpha)\); the forward-process demo reports it in decibels as it dissolves the course’s Cornell render.

See it live A real picture, dissolving: the render as \(\mathbf{x}_0\) beside \(\mathbf{x}_t\) for the \(t\) on the slider, with \(\bar\alpha\), SNR and PSNR.

6.2 The reverse process and the exact denoiser

The learned part answers a local question: given \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and \(t\), where was \(\mathbf{x}_0\)? For a finite dataset \(\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}\) the best possible answer is computable in closed form. Since \(\mathbf{x}_t\) given \(\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\) is Gaussian with mean \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\) and variance \(1-\bar\alpha\), Bayes’ rule gives the posterior mean

\[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{x}_t] \;=\; \sum_i w_i\, \mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \qquad w_i \;\propto\; \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\|^2}{2(1-\bar\alpha)}\Big), \]

a softmax-weighted average of the training points. This is what the spiral demo and the digits demo compute, exactly, and it is what a network is trained to approximate for datasets too large or too high-dimensional to average over. It is also, by Tweedie’s formula, the same thing as the score: the gradient of the log density of the noised data, \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t) = \big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 - \mathbf{x}_t\big)/(1-\bar\alpha)\), which is the vector field the spiral demo draws as arrows. Predicting the clean image, predicting the noise \(\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon} = (\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\), and predicting the score are three parameterizations of one function.

See it live The mechanism, on dots (400 points on a spiral, the exact denoiser, arrows for the score field, DDIM versus DDPM stepping), then real digits, the exact denoiser (2,000 MNIST digits as points of \(\mathbb{R}^{400}\)). In the second, every sample lands exactly on a training digit: the panel that compares each sample with its nearest training digit reads distance \(0.000\), “memorized”. An exact denoiser for a finite set can only return the set.
Worked example (two training points) Let the dataset be two scalars, \(x^{(1)} = -1\) and \(x^{(2)} = +1\), and take a time where \(\bar\alpha = \tfrac14\), so \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha} = \tfrac12\) and \(1-\bar\alpha = \tfrac34\). At \(x_t = 0.3\) the two logits are \(-\tfrac{(0.3+0.5)^2}{1.5} = -0.4267\) and \(-\tfrac{(0.3-0.5)^2}{1.5} = -0.0267\); the softmax weights are \(0.401\) and \(0.599\), and the posterior mean is \(\hat x_0 = -0.401 + 0.599 = 0.197\): a pull toward the nearer point, not a jump onto it. As \(t\to0\), \(1-\bar\alpha\to0\), the weights become one-hot, and \(\hat x_0\) becomes exactly the nearest training point, which is the memorization the digits demo shows. Keeping the kernel variance from vanishing (the demo’s smooth knob adds \(h^2\) to \(1-\bar\alpha\)) keeps the average going to the end, and the samples land between training points.

6.3 Training

The training loop is one line: pick an image \(\mathbf{x}_0\) and a random \(t\), draw \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\), form \(\mathbf{x}_t\), ask the network \(\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\) for the noise (or \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_\theta\) for the image), and minimize the squared error,

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\, t,\, \boldsymbol\epsilon} \Big[\, \big\|\boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\big\|^2 \Big]. \]

Every step is a small, well-posed regression against a target the trainer generated itself, which is why diffusion trains stably where adversarial models fought their own training. This is DDPM (Ho, Jain & Abbeel 2020); the idea comes from Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015, who borrowed it from nonequilibrium thermodynamics, and the same function was reached from the score side by Song & Ermon 2019. The course’s own network (exhibit G) is a class-conditional perceptron with about a million weights, trained by exactly this loop on 60,000 digits for fifteen minutes on a laptop CPU, predicting the clean image (tools/train-mlp.py).

6.4 Sampling

Start from \(\mathbf{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and step \(t\) down a chosen sequence \(t_K > \dots > t_0 = 0\). At each step form \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0\) from the network, recover the implied noise \(\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\), and move to the next time. The DDIM family (Song, Meng & Ermon 2021) writes the update as

\[ \mathbf{x}_{t'} \;=\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha(t')}\;\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha(t') - \sigma^2}\;\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon} \;+\; \sigma\,\mathbf{z}, \qquad \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I), \]

with \(\sigma = 0\) giving the deterministic DDIM sampler (same start, same landing, a few dozen steps) and \(\sigma\) at its DDPM value giving the original stochastic one (fresh noise each step, many steps). Fewer steps trade detail and diversity for speed; distillation trains a student to jump where the teacher walked, down to a handful of steps. Classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans 2022) trains the network both with and without its condition \(c\) and, at sampling time, exaggerates the difference by a scale \(w\): \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\varnothing) + w\,\big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(c) - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\varnothing)\big)\). Around \(w \approx 7\) prompts are followed closely; too high and images saturate and repeat.

See it live Digits nobody wrote (the smooth knob: exact is memorization, \(h\approx3\) makes novel blends, \(h=5\) collapses to one blurry average), then the network draws: the trained million-weight denoiser samples digits it never saw, with the class buttons as conditioning and guide as \(w\). Every sample’s distance to the nearest training digit is far above the memorization threshold.

7 · From digits to Stable Diffusion: the three scalings

Nothing in the recipe changes between the digit network and the largest image models; three things scale. The point gets bigger, so diffuse a latent. A \(512\times512\) image is 786,432 numbers; denoising that a thousand times per picture is unaffordable. Latent diffusion runs the whole process on the autoencoder’s \(64\times64\times4\) latent and decodes once at the end (Rombach et al. 2022); that is Stable Diffusion, and it is why the method fits on a gaming GPU. The condition gets richer, so put text in the class slot. The class embedding the digit network reads becomes a CLIP text embedding consulted at every layer through cross-attention; guidance is unchanged. The network gets bigger. U-Nets in 2020 to 2022, then transformers over image patches (DiT, Peebles & Xie 2023); quality followed scale. The two text-to-image systems that made the public notice in 2022 took different routes to the same place, DALL·E 2 diffusing in CLIP’s embedding space and Imagen conditioning on a large language model’s text embedding.

Two developments matter most to a graphics course. ControlNet (Zhang, Rao & Agrawala 2023) conditions the denoiser on a structural image, a depth map, a normal map or an edge image, and makes it keep that structure while painting the appearance; depth and normals are the pipeline’s own by-products (Sessions 6 and 7), so geometry and camera come from the renderer, where you have control, and appearance from the model. And an image model can train a 3D scene: DreamFusion (Poole et al. 2022) optimizes a NeRF so that its renders, from random viewpoints, score well under a text-conditioned image model, with the gradients flowing back through the differentiable renderer (score distillation). Video is the same recipe with a time axis: a block of latents attended across frames (Ho et al. 2022), scaled by 2024 to minute-long clips.

8 · Learned scenes: NeRF and Gaussian splatting

The other half of the neural era learns one scene from its own photographs and renders it from viewpoints the camera never saw. A neural radiance field (NeRF, Mildenhall et al. 2020) represents the scene as a function, a small network \(F_\theta : (\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)\) from a 3D point and a viewing direction to a color and a density, with no mesh anywhere. A pixel is made by marching a camera ray \(\mathbf{r}(s) = \mathbf{o} + s\,\mathbf{d}\) through it and compositing front to back:

\[ C(\mathbf{r}) \;=\; \int_{s_n}^{s_f} T(s)\, \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(s), \mathbf{d})\, ds, \qquad T(s) = \exp\!\Big(-\int_{s_n}^{s} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(u))\, du\Big), \]

where \(T\) is how much of the ray survives to depth \(s\). The integral is estimated by sampling points along the ray, and the whole march is differentiable, so training is the march run backwards: gradient descent on \(\theta\) until rendered rays match the training photographs. It is slow, hundreds of network evaluations per ray, which is why 3D Gaussian splatting (Kerbl et al. 2023) keeps the learn-from-photos idea and replaces the network with millions of explicit primitives, anisotropic Gaussians with position, covariance, view-dependent color and opacity, rendered by projecting each onto the screen and blending back to front. That is a rasterizer, so it runs in real time, and it needs the course’s view and projection matrices to do it; it is still trained by gradient descent through a differentiable renderer.

9 · What still breaks, and what graphics keeps

A diffusion model has no scene, so nothing in it enforces that the cup on frame one is the cup on frame sixty, that objects fall, that hands have five fingers, or that a sign spells a word; it has no camera to move an inch and no edit that leaves everything else fixed. Graphics has exactly those things and lacks the model’s taste for appearance. The field is merging them from both sides: engines that ship generated textures and assets, differentiable renderers that let images train scenes, and world models that generate the next frame in response to a controller input. Neither half erases the pipeline you built; both stand on it.

Exercise 1 (dimension) A 1080p RGB frame is \(1920\times1080\). What is the dimension of the vector space it lives in? If a video is 30 frames per second, what is the dimension of one second of video?
Solution \(3\cdot1920\cdot1080 = 6{,}220{,}800\). One second is 30 such vectors stacked, \(186{,}624{,}000\) coordinates: the “3D block of latents” of video diffusion is a block of exactly this shape, before the autoencoder shrinks it.
Exercise 2 (function space) Let \(f(u,v) = (u, 0, 0)\) and \(g(u,v) = (0, v, 0)\) on \([0,1]^2\). Compute \(\langle f, g\rangle\), \(\|f\|\), and \(\|f - g\|\).
Solution \(\langle f,g\rangle = \int\!\!\int (u\cdot0 + 0\cdot v + 0)\,du\,dv = 0\): the images are orthogonal (no channel overlaps). \(\|f\|^2 = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 u^2\,du\,dv = \tfrac13\), so \(\|f\| = 1/\sqrt3\); by symmetry \(\|g\| = 1/\sqrt3\), and \(\|f-g\|^2 = \|f\|^2 + \|g\|^2 = \tfrac23\).
Exercise 3 (sampling and aliasing) A gray image is \(f(u,v) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos(2\pi k u)\), a vertical stripe pattern with \(k\) periods across the picture. You sample it on an \(m\times m\) grid at pixel centers. For \(m = 8\), what do the samples look like when \(k = 8\)? When \(k = 4\)?
Solution With \(u_i = (i-\tfrac12)/8\) and \(k=8\), \(2\pi k u_i = 2\pi(i-\tfrac12)\), so \(\cos = \cos(\pi(2i-1)) = -1\) for every \(i\): the raster is uniformly black, and the stripes have vanished. Eight periods across eight pixels is exactly at the sampling rate, past the Nyquist limit of four, so the samples alias to a constant. With \(k = 4\), the samples all sit at zero crossings: \(\cos(\pi(i-\tfrac12)) = 0\) for every \(i\), a uniform mid gray, again losing the pattern (four periods on eight pixels is exactly the Nyquist limit). Any \(k < 4\) survives: \(k = 2\) gives the sample pattern \(+,-,-,+,+,-,-,+\) at amplitude \(0.707\), two periods, visibly stripes. Supersampling or a blur before sampling would remove the frequencies the grid cannot carry instead of letting them masquerade as something else.
Exercise 4 (the exact denoiser) Repeat the worked example of Section 6.2 with three training scalars \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\), \(\bar\alpha = \tfrac14\), and \(x_t = 0.3\). Which point gets the largest weight, and what is \(\hat x_0\)? Then argue, without computing, what \(\hat x_0\) becomes as \(\bar\alpha \to 0\).
Solution Logits \(-\frac{(x_t - \tfrac12 x^{(i)})^2}{1.5}\): for \(-1, 0, +1\) they are \(-0.4267, -0.0600, -0.0267\). Softmax weights \(0.254, 0.367, 0.379\); the \(+1\) point wins narrowly over \(0\). \(\hat x_0 = -0.254 + 0 + 0.379 = 0.125\). As \(\bar\alpha \to 0\) the logits all tend to \(-x_t^2/2\), the weights become equal, and \(\hat x_0\) tends to the data mean, \(0\): at high noise the best guess is the average image, which is why the first steps of every sampling run look like a grey blur.
Exercise 5 (guidance) With \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\varnothing) = (0.2, 0.2)\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(c) = (0.5, 0.1)\), compute the guided estimate for \(w = 1\), \(w = 3\), and \(w = 0\). What does \(w = 0\) mean?
Solution \(w=1\): \((0.5, 0.1)\), plain conditioning. \(w=3\): \((0.2, 0.2) + 3\,(0.3, -0.1) = (1.1, -0.1)\), the class direction exaggerated (and clamped to the valid range in practice, which is where saturation comes from). \(w=0\): \((0.2, 0.2)\), the unconditional estimate; the condition is ignored.
Exercise 6 (non-invertibility) Explain in two sentences why a network that maps a caption directly to an image (no noise, no sampling) cannot produce two different cats for “a cat”, and where a diffusion model gets the difference.
Solution A network is a function: the same input gives the same output, so a caption-to-image network returns one image per caption. A diffusion model starts each sample from a different random noise vector, which supplies the choices the caption does not specify; the caption steers, the noise decides.

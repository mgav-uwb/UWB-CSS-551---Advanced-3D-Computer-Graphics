Learned scenes: NeRF and Gaussian splatting

The other half of the neural era learns one scene from its own photographs and renders it from viewpoints the camera never saw. A neural radiance field (NeRF, Mildenhall et al. 2020) represents the scene as a function, a small network \(F_\theta : (\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{d}) \mapsto (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)\) from a 3D point and a viewing direction to a color and a density, with no mesh anywhere. A pixel is made by marching a camera ray \(\mathbf{r}(s) = \mathbf{o} + s\,\mathbf{d}\) through it and compositing front to back:

\[ C(\mathbf{r}) \;=\; \int_{s_n}^{s_f} T(s)\, \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(s), \mathbf{d})\, ds, \qquad T(s) = \exp\!\Big(-\int_{s_n}^{s} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(u))\, du\Big), \]

where \(T\) is how much of the ray survives to depth \(s\). The integral is estimated by sampling points along the ray, and the whole march is differentiable, so training is the march run backwards: gradient descent on \(\theta\) until rendered rays match the training photographs. It is slow, hundreds of network evaluations per ray, which is why 3D Gaussian splatting (Kerbl et al. 2023) keeps the learn-from-photos idea and replaces the network with millions of explicit primitives, anisotropic Gaussians with position, covariance, view-dependent color and opacity, rendered by projecting each onto the screen and blending back to front. That is a rasterizer, so it runs in real time, and it needs the course’s view and projection matrices to do it; it is still trained by gradient descent through a differentiable renderer.

See it live A real splat scene: the “bonsai” capture from the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset, about 240,000 Gaussians rendered live in the browser (attribution in media/gsplat/ATTRIBUTION.md).

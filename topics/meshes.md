## Triangles: always planar, always convex

Every real-time surface, a character, a terrain, a car, is a **triangle mesh**. Two reasons the hardware insists:

- a triangle is always **planar**: three points define exactly one plane, so the surface between them is unambiguous
- a triangle is always **convex**: three edge functions decide inside or outside, the fixed test the GPU runs billions of times a second

A quad's four corners can be non-planar (bent), and its fill is ambiguous. Split it into two triangles and both problems vanish.


---

## A mesh is two arrays

<img src="../../textbook/figures/mshd-two-arrays.svg" style="height:380px" alt="   vertices v[]         indices t[]  (triples)
     v0 = (…)             t = [ 0, 3, 1,   ← triangle 0
     v1 = (…)                   1, 3, 4,   ← triangle 1
     v2 = (…)                   … ]">

- a **vertex array**: the positions (and later normals, UVs), each vertex stored **once**
- an **index array**: flat triples, each naming one triangle by pointing into the vertex array


---

## Winding picks the front face

<img src="../../textbook/figures/mshd-winding.svg" style="height:300px" alt="WebGL, seen from above: (0, 3, 1) runs counter-clockwise, front face, drawn.
WebGL, seen from below: (0, 3, 1) runs clockwise, back face, culled.
Unity, seen from above, +z up the page: (0, 3, 1) runs clockwise, Unity's front face, drawn.">

- index order decides which side is the **front**; the **back** is usually culled
- WebGL's front is **counter-clockwise**; Unity's is **clockwise**, with z flipped, so the **same triples face up in both**
- wrong order: the triangle vanishes, or lights from the inside

Two quads per side make a **3×3 lattice**, numbered **row by row** (row along z, column along x). Four quads, split in two, make **eight triangles**.

## Row-major indexing

For an `$n \times n$` grid, the vertex at (`row`, `col`), both running `$0 \ldots n$`, lives at one formula:

<div class="tex" role="img" aria-label="index(row, col) = row · (n + 1) + col">
\[ \text{index}(\text{row}, \text{col}) = \text{row} \cdot (n + 1) + \text{col} \]
</div>

At `$n = 2$` that is `$3 \cdot \text{row} + \text{col}$`: (0, 0) is 0, (1, 1) is 4, (2, 2) is 8. One quad sits at each (`row`, `col`) for both in `$0 \ldots n-1$`, with four corners:

<div class="tex" role="img" aria-label="v00 = index(row, col)      v01 = index(row, col+1)
v10 = index(row+1, col)    v11 = index(row+1, col+1)">
\[ \begin{aligned} v_{00} &= \text{index}(\text{row},\ \text{col}) & \qquad v_{01} &= \text{index}(\text{row},\ \text{col}+1) \\ v_{10} &= \text{index}(\text{row}+1,\ \text{col}) & \qquad v_{11} &= \text{index}(\text{row}+1,\ \text{col}+1) \end{aligned} \]
</div>

Every triangle is written in these four names: no magic numbers.

## The half-edge structure

- links: `to`, `face`, `next` (around the face), `twin` (the opposite half)

## Euler's formula checks a mesh

For a closed surface, `$V - E + F = 2 - 2g$` (`$g$` = holes through it); each boundary loop subtracts 1 more.

| mesh | `$V - E + F$` | | |
| ---- | ------------- | - | - |
| cube | `$8 - 12 + 6$` | 2 | closed, genus 0 |
| the 2×2 grid | `$9 - 16 + 8$` | 1 | one boundary loop |
| icosphere | `$162 - 480 + 320$` | 2 | closed, genus 0 |
| Stanford bunny | `$34{,}834 - 104{,}288 + 69{,}451$` | −3 | genus 0, so 5 boundary loops |

The bunny has **five holes in its base** (loops of 80, 42, 40, 39 and 22 edges): where the scanner could not see.

## Valence: meshes are mostly sixes

<img src="../../textbook/figures/mesh-valence.svg" class="media-shot" style="height:240px" alt="histogram of vertex valence on the Stanford bunny, peaked sharply at six: valence 6 for 75.1 % of vertices, 5 or 7 for 22.5 %, mean 5.988">

<div class="tex" style="font-size:0.72em" role="img" aria-label="every triangle has 3 edges, every edge 2 triangles: 3F = 2E. V − E + F ≈ 0 gives E ≈ 3V and F ≈ 2V, so the mean valence 2E/V ≈ 6">
\[ 3F = 2E, \quad V - E + F \approx 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; E \approx 3V,\ \ F \approx 2V \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{mean valence} = \frac{2E}{V} \approx 6 \]
</div>

- the bunny: valence 6 for **75.1 %** of vertices, 5 or 7 for 22.5 %, mean **5.988**
- irregular vertices (4, 5, 7, 8) are where subdivision and remeshing leave artifacts

### Normals, and a mesh from a profile

<small>(~14 min)</small>

## Normals: which way the surface faces

Positions give a surface its **shape**; they say nothing about which way it **faces**. Lighting needs that direction at every point:

- brightness depends on the angle between the **normal** and the light (the illumination topic)
- a position array with no normals renders **unlit**: a silhouette, no shading

So every vertex carries a **unit normal** beside its position. The question is where it comes from, and the answer is a cross product.


---

## A face normal is a cross product

A triangle is flat, so it has **one** normal. Cross two edges from a shared corner:

<div class="tex" role="img" aria-label="faceN = (v1 − v0) × (v2 − v0)">
\[ \mathbf{n}_{\text{face}} = (v_1 - v_0) \times (v_2 - v_0) \]
</div>

The **winding** fixes the sign: listed front-facing, the cross points out the front. The first triangle `$(0, 3, 1)$` of the flat grid:

<div class="tex" style="font-size:0.74em" role="img" aria-label="edge1 = v3 − v0 = (0, 0, 1.5)      edge2 = v1 − v0 = (1.5, 0, 0)
faceN = edge1 × edge2 = (0, 2.25, 0)   →   normalize → (0, 1, 0)">
\[ \begin{aligned} e_1 &= v_3 - v_0 = (0,\ 0,\ 1.5) \qquad e_2 = v_1 - v_0 = (1.5,\ 0,\ 0) \\ e_1 \times e_2 &= (0,\ 2.25,\ 0) \;\to\; (0,\ 1,\ 0) \end{aligned} \]
</div>

The cross's **length is twice the triangle's area**: `$2.25 = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 (1.5)(1.5)$`.


---

## From face normals to vertex normals

Each interior vertex is shared by several faces. Its normal is the normalized **sum** of theirs:

<div class="tex" role="img" aria-label="n[i] = normalize( Σ faceN(f) )     over every face f that touches vertex i">
\[ \mathbf{n}_i = \text{normalize}\Big( \sum_{f \,\ni\, i} \mathbf{n}_{\text{face}}(f) \Big) \]
</div>

<div class="code-tabs">

```csharp
Vector3[] sums = new Vector3[v.Length];
for (int t = 0; t < tri.Length; t += 3) {
    int a = tri[t], b = tri[t + 1], c = tri[t + 2];
    Vector3 face = Vector3.Cross(v[b] - v[a], v[c] - v[a]);   // length = 2 x area
    sums[a] += face;   sums[b] += face;   sums[c] += face;
}
```

```javascript
const sums = v.map(() => [0, 0, 0]);
for (let t = 0; t < tri.length; t += 3) {
  const a = tri[t], b = tri[t + 1], c = tri[t + 2];
  const face = cross(sub(v[b], v[a]), sub(v[c], v[a]));       // length = 2 x area
  sums[a] = add(sums[a], face);  sums[b] = add(sums[b], face);  sums[c] = add(sums[c], face);
}
```

</div>

Unnormalized crosses give a bigger face a bigger vote. HW5 and the demo do this; Sung's code normalizes each face first, so every face votes equally.

## Sweeping a profile into a surface

<div style="display:flex; align-items:center; gap:10px; justify-content:center">
<img src="../../textbook/figures/mesh-sweep.svg" class="media-shot" style="height:300px" alt="a profile polyline beside the rotation axis, and the wireframe surface of revolution it sweeps, rings connected by meridians">
<div class="tex" style="font-size:0.66em" role="img" aria-label="vertex(i, j) = R_y(θ_j) · profile[i],   θ_j = j · Δθ,   Δθ = 2π / S

</div>
</div>

**P** profile points and **S** rotation steps give a `$P \times S$` grid of vertices, in quads like the flat grid. The index `$j$` wraps modulo `$S$`, so the surface closes.

## Degenerate case: the poles

<img src="../../textbook/figures/mshd-pole.svg" style="height:290px" alt="quads to the axis: 16 copies of the pole, 112 vertices, 192 triangles, 16 of them zero-area with no normal. a triangle fan: 1 vertex at the pole, 97 vertices, 176 triangles. P = 7 profile points, S = 16 steps">

- a profile point **on** the axis has radius 0: all `$S$` rotated copies land on one point, and each quad there has a **zero-area** triangle, whose cross is the zero vector
- fix: collapse the pole to **one** vertex and cap it with a **fan**
- guard: a vertex normal whose sum is near zero becomes `$(0, 1, 0)$`, never a `NaN`

## Simplification: collapse an edge

<img src="../../textbook/figures/mesh-edge-collapse.svg" class="media-shot" style="height:250px" alt="an edge collapsed: its two endpoints merged into one vertex, the two triangles on the edge removed">

## The quadric error, worked

Garland and Heckbert (1997): the cost of moving a vertex to `$p$` is the sum of squared distances to the planes of its original triangles, `$\sum (\mathbf{n}_k \cdot p + d_k)^2$`.

The lifted 2×2 grid, edge 4–1 (center to an edge vertex), seven planes:

| merge at | cost |
| -------- | ---- |
| vertex 4 | 0.160 |
| vertex 1 | 1.457 |
| the midpoint | 0.404 |
| the optimum (0, 0.338, 0), from a 3×3 system | **0.135** |

Edge 0–1, on the flat border: merge at vertex 1, cost **0**. Each vertex keeps one 4×4 matrix, the sum of its planes' quadrics; a collapse adds two matrices. Cheap enough for millions of edges.

## Mesh files: OBJ

Wavefront **OBJ**: text, one vertex or face per line, indices starting at **1**. The lifted 2×2 grid:

```text
   v -1.5 0 -1.5        f 1 4 2
   v  0   0 -1.5        f 2 4 5
   v  1.5 0 -1.5        f 2 5 3
   v -1.5 0  0          f 3 5 6
   v  0   0.4 0         f 4 7 5
   v  1.5 0  0          f 5 7 8
   v -1.5 0  1.5        f 5 8 6
   v  0   0  1.5        f 6 8 9
   v  1.5 0  1.5
```

- `f 1 4 2` is the triangle `(0, 3, 1)`: add 1 to every index
- normals and texture coordinates are separate lists, joined per corner as `f 1/1/1 4/4/4 2/2/2`
- no binary form, no scene graph, no animation; readable everywhere

### Learned scenes: NeRF and Gaussian splatting

<small>(~94 min)</small>


---

## A different question: inverse rendering

Everything so far is **forward** rendering: **you build** the geometry and materials, then the pipeline projects and shades them into pixels. The frontier flips the arrow:

```text
   forward:   scene (meshes, materials, lights)  ──render──▶  image
   inverse:   many photographs  ──optimize──▶  a 3D scene you can re-render
```

- **inverse rendering / novel-view synthesis**: given **photos** of a real scene from different angles, **recover** a 3D representation, then render it from **new** viewpoints the camera never saw
- the representation is **learned by optimization** (gradient descent), not modeled by an artist
- the breakthroughs of the last few years, **NeRF** (neural radiance fields, 2020) and **3D Gaussian splatting** (2023), are both this: **photographs in, a re-renderable 3D scene out**


---

## The methods, at a glance

| method | problem it solves | the idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **structure from motion** | where was each photo taken? | match features across photos; solve for the cameras |
| **NeRF** (2020) | photos to new views | the scene is a network; a pixel is a march along a ray |
| **Instant-NGP**, **Mip-NeRF**, **NeuS** | slow, shimmering, foggy | a lookup table; cones; distances to a surface |
| **3D Gaussian splatting** (2023) | NeRF renders in seconds | millions of fuzzy blobs, rasterized in real time |
| **marching cubes** (1987) | a field is not a mesh | threshold the field, a table per cell |

- who uses them: phone capture apps and web viewers (splats), mapping and street-level 3D, film and game set capture, product scans


---

## Where the poses come from: structure from motion

Before any learning, a **structure-from-motion** program (COLMAP, Schönberger & Frahm 2016) finds the same features in many photos and solves for every camera's position and orientation, plus a sparse cloud of 3D points.

- its core step is **triangulation**: two cameras see the same feature, and the 3D point is where their two rays meet
- a small matching error moves the point: little when the rays meet at a **wide angle**, a lot when they are nearly parallel
- every NeRF and splat scene starts from these poses; bad poses make **blur that no training fixes**


---

## Capturing a scene well

- **walk around** the object: an orbit with a photo every few degrees, at two or three heights, a couple of hundred photos; neighboring photos overlap almost entirely
- lock **exposure and white balance**: the scene has one color per point and direction, so a brightness change between photos reads as a change in the scene
- nothing may **move**: a person walking through becomes a smear or a floater
- sharp, textured surfaces match well; glass, mirrors and blank walls do not


---

## NeRF: the scene is a function

**NeRF**, a neural radiance field (Mildenhall et al. 2020): the **entire scene** is one small network, **no mesh**:

```text
   F(p, d) = (c, σ)      in:  a 3D point p and a viewing direction d
                         out: the color c there, and the density σ (sigma): how strongly the stuff there absorbs a ray
```

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-nerf-ray.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 190px;" alt="a camera shoots a ray through a translucent blob; seven sample points along the ray, three inside the blob; a box names the network F of position and direction, another the compositing formulas">

- density is **zero in air** and large inside a leaf: the network has learned **where the stuff is**
- one network per scene, about 600,000 weights, run at **every sample of every ray**


---

## The view direction enters late

- the density depends on the **position only**; the color depends on position **and** direction
- if density could depend on the view direction, a surface could be **solid from one side and empty from another**: every photo could be explained by its own geometry, and the geometry would be wrong
- keeping the direction out of the density forces **one** geometry to agree with all the photos; the direction may only tint the color, which is how glints and highlights appear


---

## Positional encoding: give the network frequencies

Fed raw coordinates, a small network makes **blurry** fields: it prefers smooth functions, and a scene has edges. NeRF first turns every coordinate into sines and cosines at doubling frequencies:

```text
   γ(p) = ( sin πp, cos πp,  sin 2πp, cos 2πp,  sin 4πp, cos 4πp,  … )
```

- γ (gamma) is the encoding, π (pi) the usual 3.14159: one number becomes many, from slow waves to fast ones
- two nearby points look alike in the slow entries and very different in the fast ones, so the network can put an **edge** between them: the cosine basis of the image-space lecture again
- too few frequencies: blur; too many: the network fits noise between the photos and **flickers** in new views (NeRF uses 10 for the position, 4 for the direction)


---

## The camera ray for a pixel

Every pixel of every photo is a **ray**: from the camera's position, through the pixel, into the scene.

- it is the viewing lecture's projection **run backwards**: the pixel's offset from the image center, divided by the focal length, is a direction in camera space; the camera's pose turns it into the world
- image rows count **down** while the camera's y counts **up**: one sign flip, as in the viewport transform
- training draws random pixels from all the photos; each brings its ray and the color it should produce


---

## Rendering a field: march and composite

March along the ray in steps of length δ (delta). The network gives each sample a density σᵢ and a color cᵢ:

```text
   αᵢ = 1 − exp(−σᵢ δ)                  the sample's opacity: the share of arriving light it stops
   Tᵢ = (1 − α₁)(1 − α₂) … (1 − αᵢ₋₁)   the transmittance: the share of the ray still unblocked there
   C  = Σ Tᵢ αᵢ cᵢ                      the pixel: each sample's color, weighted by Tᵢ αᵢ
```

- α (alpha) is the opacity; Σ (capital sigma) means "sum over the samples"; read the last line front to back: **what is left of the ray, times how much this sample stops, times its color**
- the alpha compositing of the texturing lecture, run front to back; renderRay in Homework 8 (HW8) is this loop


---

## Occlusion without a depth buffer

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-volume-render.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 260px;" alt="the transmittance stepping down from 1 to 0.07 along five samples with the per-sample weights as bars peaking at samples 3 and 4, and a red swatch of the resulting pixel color">

- a dense sample drives T toward **zero**: everything behind it gets almost **no weight**
- no depth test and no sorting of surfaces: **occlusion is the product** of what lies in front
- the weights add up to 1 − T(end), one minus the transmittance left after the last sample


---

## Opacity depends on the step

The same stuff, sampled more finely, must not become more opaque:

```text
   σ = 4:   one step of 0.2   →  α = 0.551
            two steps of 0.1  →  α = 0.330 each;  together 1 − (1 − 0.330)² = 0.551
```

- this is why NeRF stores **density**, a property of the stuff, not **opacity**, a property of the stuff **and** the step
- a fine pass adds samples without adding opacity


---

## The background behind the ray

Whatever transmittance is left after the last sample reaches the background:

```text
   pixel = C + T(end) · background
```

- NeRF's synthetic scenes are trained **over white** (`white_bkgd = True` in the original code)
- train over the wrong background and the network paints it into the object: a **halo** of gray or white


---

## A depth for free

- the weights Tᵢαᵢ say **where along the ray** the pixel's color came from
- the weighted average of the sample depths is a **depth**: a NeRF renders a depth map with no extra work
- that depth is linear in distance, unlike a depth buffer: what ControlNet's depth condition wants (the diffusion lectures)


---

## Pitfall: a thin surface between samples

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-thin-surface.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 230px;" alt="a thin slab along the ray with three samplings; both coarse samplings miss it, and the shifted one that hits it overshoots to full opacity">

- samples that **straddle** a leaf thinner than the step miss it: **invisible**
- a sample that lands **inside** it is taken to fill the whole step: **far too opaque**
- which one happens depends on where the samples fall, so thin structure **flickers** from ray to ray


---

## Where to put the samples

- **jitter**: split the ray into bins and draw one random depth in each, a new one at every training step, so that over training every depth gets tested (the antialiasing lecture's jittered sampling)
- **coarse to fine**: a coarse pass finds where the weights are large; a fine pass puts most of its samples there
- the fine pass is the **importance sampling** of the ray-tracing lecture, with the coarse pass supplying the importance; NeRF uses 64 coarse and 128 fine samples per ray


---

## Training: the march run backwards

Every step of the march is **differentiable**: the network, the exponentials, the products, the sum. So gradient descent can adjust the network until rendered pixels match the photos:

- the loss: for every pixel of every photo, the squared difference between the rendered and the photographed color, added up
- nothing says where the surfaces are: the network **discovers** that putting density there makes rays from many viewpoints agree
- a density change is a **trade**: the sample gains weight, everything behind it loses weight
- the cost: hundreds of network evaluations per ray, thousands of rays per step, hundreds of thousands of steps


---

## What training cannot see

- a region **no photograph looked at** is unconstrained: it fills with whatever density is convenient, the **floaters**, wisps of density in the air of a reconstructed room
- a scene that **changes between photographs** (a person walking through, the sun moving) disagrees with itself and comes out as a **smear**
- **lighting is baked in**: the field reproduces the light that was there; move a lamp and nothing changes
- the original method: one to two days on one GPU (graphics processing unit) per scene; about thirty seconds per 800 × 800 frame


---

## Faster: a hash grid instead of a big network

Instant-NGP, instant neural graphics primitives (Müller et al. 2022), moves the capacity **from the network into a lookup table**:

- grids at several resolutions hold learned numbers; a point blends the numbers stored at the grid corners around it, and a **tiny network** decodes them
- the fine grids have more corners than the table has entries, so corners **share** entries (a hash); the corners near surfaces win, because empty space does not care
- a sample reads a few dozen numbers instead of running 600,000 multiply-adds: training fell from a day to **minutes**


---

## Aliasing: cones, not rays

A pixel sees a **cone** that widens with distance; NeRF samples an infinitely thin ray, so a scene trained close up **shimmers** when seen from far away.

- Mip-NeRF (Barron et al. 2021; "mip" as in mipmap) encodes a small **region** of space instead of a point
- frequencies finer than the region **fade out** automatically: the antialiasing lecture's prefilter, applied to the network's input
- the same scene renders without shimmer at every distance


---

## Unbounded scenes: squeeze the far away

- outdoor captures have a background at any distance; a grid or an encoding over all of space wastes its resolution on emptiness
- Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron et al. 2022) leaves the near region alone and **squeezes** everything farther into a thin shell: all of space beyond 10 units lands in the outer 5 % of the ball's radius
- detail falls off as one over the distance, like a depth buffer; the paper reports a 57 % lower squared error than Mip-NeRF on such scenes


---

## Surfaces instead of fog: NeuS

A density field is fog: it has no crisp surface to extract.

- NeuS, neural implicit surfaces (Wang et al. 2021), stores the **signed distance** to the surface instead: negative inside, zero on it, positive outside
- the opacity is derived from it so that it switches on in a **thin band** around the zero, and training narrows the band from fog to a surface
- the surface is exactly where the distance is zero; marching cubes turns it into a clean mesh


---

## 3D Gaussian splatting: back to primitives

**3D Gaussian splatting** (3DGS; Kerbl et al. 2023) keeps "learn it from photos" and drops the slow network. The scene is **millions of 3D Gaussians**, fuzzy translucent blobs, each with:

```text
   a center μ (mu)                  where it is
   a shape Σ, a 3 × 3 matrix        an oriented, stretched ellipsoid
   a color                          that changes with the view direction
   an opacity                       how solid it is
```

- render by **projecting** each blob to the screen and **blending** the footprints in depth order: a **rasterizer**, not a ray march, so it runs in **real time**
- trained the same way: gradient descent moves, stretches, recolors and fades the blobs until the renders match the photos, and **adds or removes** blobs as needed
- the project-and-rasterize pipeline of this course, with a fuzzy blob in place of a triangle


---

## A splat's footprint on the screen

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-splat-projection.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 230px;" alt="a splat's one, two and three standard-deviation ellipses on the screen at depth 2, solid, and at depth 4, dashed, half the size">

- a 3D Gaussian seen through a pinhole camera is a 2D Gaussian: an **ellipse** of fading opacity
- its size scales with the focal length over the depth: the same splat twice as far away is half as wide
- long, thin ellipses are how a splat scene draws an **edge** or a **leaf**


---

## Projecting a splat: the formulas HW8 uses

Given, not derived. A splat has scales s and a rotation R; its center is t = (x, y, z) in camera space, the focal length f:

```text
   Σ  = M Mᵀ,   M = R · diag(s)          the 3D shape: a unit sphere scaled, then rotated
   J  = [ f/z   0     −f·x/z² ]           how the projection stretches a small patch at t
        [ 0     f/z   −f·y/z² ]           (HW8: the pinhole Jacobian)
   Σ′ = J Σ Jᵀ = [ a  b ]                  the 2 × 2 shape on the screen
                 [ b  c ]
   center (f·x/z, f·y/z);   m = (a + c)/2,   h = √( ((a − c)/2)² + b² )
   radii √(m + h) and √(m − h);   angle ½ · atan2(2b, a − c)
```

- a scale and a rotation are stored instead of Σ itself, so that **any** values the optimizer picks give a valid ellipsoid


---

## Which pixels a splat touches

- every footprint gets a small **floor**: 0.3 square pixels added to its spread, so no splat is thinner than about half a pixel and none falls between pixel centers and vanishes
- the screen is cut into **16 × 16-pixel tiles**; a splat is listed in every tile that its ellipse, out to three radii, overlaps
- a big splat lands in hundreds of tiles and is expensive; a small one in one to four
- each tile walks its list front to back and stops once almost nothing of the ray is left


---

## Sort once per frame

- every (splat, tile) entry gets one key: the **tile number first, then the depth**
- **one** sort on the GPU orders all of them every frame, from the current camera: grouped by tile, front to back inside each tile
- the order is by **splat center**, not per pixel: two overlapping splats at nearly the same depth can blend in the wrong order (two slides on)


---

## Blending: the march's rule, with splats

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-splat.svg" style="width: 256px; height: 240px; object-fit: cover; object-position: right center;" alt="three colored splats along one pixel row, red in front, blue, then yellow, and their front-to-back composite">

```text
   αₖ = opacityₖ · exp(−(u − μₖ)² / (2 sₖ²))      the splat's opacity, faded by the distance from its center
   C  = Σ Tₖ αₖ cₖ                                 front to back, as in the march
```

- a splat whose footprint does not reach the pixel adds nothing, however far forward it is
- HW8's compositeSplats does this along one row of pixels: u the pixel's position, μₖ and sₖ the splat's center and spread


---

## Pitfall: popping when the order flips

Two overlapping splats at almost the same depth, red and green, each with `α = 0.7`:

```text
   red in front:     weights red 0.7, green 0.21      the pixel is mostly red
   green in front:   weights red 0.21, green 0.7      the pixel is mostly green
```

- the sort uses **centers**; turn the camera a little and the centers swap order: the pixel **jumps** from red to green in one frame
- visible as flicker on thin, overlapping splats; later methods sort per pixel or per piece of ray


---

## Color that changes with the view

A leaf looks different from different sides. Each splat stores its color as a few numbers per direction pattern (**spherical harmonics**: the cosine-basis idea on the sphere of directions):

| degree | numbers per splat (red, green, blue) | what it can show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 3 | one color from every side: matte |
| 1 | 12 | broad shading |
| 3 | 48 | highlights; the paper's choice |

- 48 of a splat's 59 numbers are color: **81 % of the memory** is view dependence, not geometry
- view dependence is **captured, not simulated**: there is no light in the scene; the highlights came from the photos


---

## Training a splat scene

| ingredient | what the paper does |
| ---------- | ------------------- |
| start | one Gaussian per point of the structure-from-motion cloud |
| loss | per-pixel error plus a structure term, SSIM (structural similarity), render against photo |
| densify | where a Gaussian is pulled hard, add Gaussians (next slide) |
| prune | nearly transparent Gaussians are deleted; now and then every opacity is reset near zero, so floaters must earn their place again |
| length | 30,000 iterations, under an hour per scene |

- the **number** of primitives changes during training: the bonsai in the demo ended with about 240,000


---

## Clone or split

Every few hundred iterations, each Gaussian whose **screen-space position gradient** stays large (the optimizer keeps pulling it) is densified:

- a **small** one is **cloned**: a copy, moved along the pull; the region needs more blobs
- a **large** one is **split** into two smaller ones: one blob is covering detail it cannot represent
- either way, a large pull means the Gaussian **cannot fit its region alone**; the count grows from the structure-from-motion points to hundreds of thousands


---

## Pitfall: zooming out, and Mip-Splatting

- zoom out and a splat's true footprint shrinks below the 0.3-square-pixel floor; the floor then sets its size
- thin structures look **fat and bright** from far away: the floor fixes aliasing at the training distance and causes it at others
- Mip-Splatting (Yu et al. 2023) ties the smoothing to how finely the training photos saw each region, the idea of Mip-NeRF's cones

## From a field back to a mesh

A density or distance field defines a surface **implicitly**: where the field crosses a threshold. **Marching cubes** (Lorensen & Cline 1987; marching squares in 2D) turns it into triangles:

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-marching-squares.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 170px;" alt="marching squares on a two-blob density field at threshold 0.5: 22 of the 144 cells crossed, each contributing one segment">

- each cell compares its corners to the threshold and looks up which edges the surface crosses: a cube's 8 corners give 256 cases, one table
- the mesh inherits the field's faults: floaters become little shells, thin parts vanish; some cells can be joined two ways, and inconsistent choices leave **cracks**


---

## Flat splats for surfaces: 2DGS

2D Gaussian splatting (2DGS; Huang et al. 2024) makes every primitive a **flat disk**: two scales and a rotation, no thickness.

- a disk has a **normal**: the geometry becomes a surface, not a fog, and depth and normal maps come out clean
- it is rendered by an exact ray-disk intersection instead of the projected ellipse
- the trade: somewhat lower image quality than 3D splats, much better meshes


---

## Mesh, NeRF, splats

| | mesh | NeRF | 3D Gaussian splatting |
| - | ---- | ---- | --------------------- |
| the scene is | vertices, triangles, textures, materials | a network F(p, d) | a list of Gaussians |
| authored by | a modeler, or a scan plus cleanup | photos and a day of optimization | photos and under an hour of optimization |
| rendered by | rasterization | ray marching, hundreds of calls per pixel | rasterization of sorted splats |
| lighting | computed; relightable | baked into view-dependent color | baked into view-dependent color |
| editable | vertex by vertex | with difficulty | splat by splat, coarsely |

On the Mip-NeRF 360 benchmark (Kerbl et al. 2023): Mip-NeRF 360 reaches 27.69 decibels (dB) of PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio) in about 48 hours of training at 0.06 frames per second; 3DGS 27.21 dB in 41 minutes at 134 frames per second, 734 megabytes per scene.

## Judging a learned scene

Hold out some photographs, render their views, compare:

| metric | measures | better |
| ------ | -------- | ------ |
| PSNR | per-pixel squared error, in decibels | higher |
| SSIM | local structure: means, contrasts, correlations in small windows | higher |
| LPIPS (learned perceptual image patch similarity) | distance between a deep network's features of the two images | lower |

- half a decibel of PSNR is about 12 % more squared error: small; the benchmark's gap in **speed** (0.06 against 134 frames per second) is not

## Learned scene, learned image

```text
   LEARNED SCENE (NeRF, 3DGS)              LEARNED IMAGE (diffusion)
   ────────────────────────────            ─────────────────────────
   one scene, from its own photos          all images, from an archive
   a camera you can move                   no camera, no scene
   renders new VIEWS of the same thing     samples NEW things
   a function of 3D position (a field)     a function of a noisy image (a denoiser)
   trained through a differentiable        trained by predicting noise;
   renderer (your V and P inside it)       renders nothing itself
   meet: score distillation (diffusion)    meet: ControlNet on your depth buffer
```

- 3D Gaussian splatting literally **rasterizes with a projection matrix**: it needs your **V** and **P**
- "shade a point, accumulate along a ray, composite front to back" is the same idea with a triangle, a network sample, or a Gaussian
- neither half erases the pipeline; both stand on it

### Learned scenes: NeRF and 3DGS

<small>(~45 min)</small>

Note: One scene, learned from its own photographs, rendered from viewpoints the camera never saw. Two methods carry the arc: NeRF, a scene as a network, rendered by an integral you will compute by hand along one ray; and Gaussian splatting, the same idea returned to explicit primitives and an ordinary rasterizer, which is why the real scene at the end runs live in the browser. The reading is the learned-scenes chapter of the course text, where every number on these slides is worked.

---

## A different question: inverse rendering

Everything so far is **forward** rendering: **you build** the geometry and materials, then the pipeline projects and shades them into pixels. The frontier flips the arrow:

```text
   forward:   scene (meshes, materials, lights)  ──render──▶  image
   inverse:   many photographs  ──optimize──▶  a 3D scene you can re-render
```

- **inverse rendering / novel-view synthesis** — given a set of **photos** of a real scene from different angles, **recover** a 3D representation, then render it from **new** viewpoints the camera never saw
- the representation is **learned by optimization** (gradient descent), not modeled by an artist
- the last few years' breakthroughs — **NeRF**, then **3D Gaussian splatting** — are both this: **photographs in, a re-renderable 3D scene out**

Note: Frame the whole topic as a reversal of the arrow the course has followed. Everything up to now is FORWARD rendering: a human builds the scene, models the meshes, paints the textures, sets the lights and materials, and the pipeline transforms and shades that scene into an image. The frontier that has moved fastest in the last few years asks the INVERSE question: start from a set of ordinary PHOTOGRAPHS of a real scene taken from many angles, and RECOVER a 3D representation that, when rendered, reproduces those photos and can then be rendered from entirely new viewpoints. The defining move is that the 3D representation is not authored by hand; it is LEARNED by optimization: define a renderer that is differentiable, then run gradient descent to nudge the scene parameters until the rendered images match the training photographs, the same loop that trains neural networks. Two landmark methods define the arc: NeRF in 2020, which represents the scene as a network, and 3D Gaussian splatting in 2023, which represents it as millions of primitives and renders in real time. Next: NeRF.

## NeRF: the scene is a function

**NeRF** (Mildenhall et al. 2020): the **entire scene** is one small network, **no mesh**:

```text
   F(p, d) = (c, σ)      p = a 3D point,  d = viewing direction
                         c = color there,  σ = density: how much a ray is absorbed per unit length
```

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-nerf-ray.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 190px;" alt="a camera shoots a ray through a translucent blob; seven sample points along the ray, three inside the blob; a box names the network F of position and direction, another the compositing formulas">

- density is **zero in air**, large inside a leaf: the network has learned **where the stuff is**; color depends on the view direction too, so glints come for free
- the **image as a function** idea applied to a volume: eight layers of 256 units, **one scene per network**

Note: The scene is a function from a 3D point and a viewing direction to a color and a density, and that function is a small network; there is no mesh anywhere. Density is the new word: how much of a ray passing through a point is absorbed per unit length, zero in empty air, large inside a leaf. Where the network has put density is where the network has decided the scene has stuff. Color depends on the direction as well, which is how a NeRF reproduces a glint on a glossy surface without a material model. Connect to the foundations topic: a network is a function; here the function is the scene. Next: one detail that makes it sharp.

## Positional encoding: give the network frequencies

Fed raw coordinates, a small network makes **blurry** fields: a perceptron prefers smooth functions and a scene has edges. NeRF first maps every coordinate through fixed sines and cosines:

```text
   γ(p) = ( sin(πp), cos(πp),  sin(2πp), cos(2πp),  sin(4πp), cos(4πp),  …  up to 2^(L−1) )

   p = 0.3, L = 4:    k=0: 0.809, 0.588    k=1: 0.951, −0.309
                      k=2: −0.588, −0.809  k=3: 0.951, 0.309
```

- one number became eight; two points 0.01 apart now differ a lot in the high-frequency entries (`cos(8π·0.31) = 0.063` against `0.309`)
- it is the **cosine basis** of the space-of-images chapter again: the network combines many frequencies and can place an edge
- NeRF uses L = 10 for position (60 numbers) and L = 4 for direction

Note: The one implementation detail worth the time, because it connects to the sampling and bases discussion of the image-space chapter. Fed raw x, y, z, a perceptron produces smooth, blurry fields; scenes have edges. The fix is to feed the network sines and cosines of the coordinates at doubling frequencies, so that it is combining basis functions of many frequencies rather than bending a few tanh steps. Read the worked row: p = 0.3 becomes eight numbers, and a point 0.01 away has a very different eighth entry, which is what lets the network put an edge between them. Next: how a pixel is made from the field.

## Rendering a field: the volume integral

A pixel is made by **marching a camera ray** `r(s) = o + s·d` through the field and compositing front to back:

```text
   C(r) = ∫ T(s) · σ(r(s)) · c(r(s), d) ds        T(s) = exp( − ∫ σ(r(u)) du )   (the transmittance:
          ─────────────────────────────                     ────────────────      how much of the ray survives to depth s)

   sampled at points s₁ < … < sN, spacing δ:
   αᵢ = 1 − exp(−σᵢ δᵢ)        Tᵢ = ∏ (1 − αⱼ) over j < i        C = Σ Tᵢ αᵢ cᵢ
```

- αᵢ is the **opacity of the i-th slab**, Tᵢ how much of the ray is **still unblocked** when it gets there, Tᵢαᵢ the slab's **weight** in the pixel
- this is the **alpha compositing** of the texturing lecture, run front to back; the weights sum to `1 − T(end)`, the rest is background
- no depth buffer, no sorting of surfaces: **occlusion is a product of transmittances**

Note: The integral, then its discrete form, which is all anyone computes. Read T first: the transmittance is the fraction of the ray that has not been absorbed by depth s, an exponential of the accumulated density, and the integrand says "what reaches depth s, times how much is absorbed there, times what color it is". The discrete form is a front-to-back alpha compositing: each sample is a slab with opacity alpha, the running product T is how much of the ray is left, and each slab contributes T times alpha times its color. Say the two consequences on the slide: the weights sum to one minus the final transmittance, so what is left goes to the background; and occlusion needs no depth buffer and no sorting, because a dense slab in front drives T to zero for everything behind it. Next: do it by hand.

## One ray, five samples, by hand

Spacing δ = 0.2. The network returned these densities and colors:

```text
   i   σᵢ    color            αᵢ = 1 − e^(−0.2σᵢ)     Tᵢ                 wᵢ = Tᵢαᵢ
   1   0     (air)            1 − e^0    = 0          1                  0
   2   0.5   gray  (.5,.5,.5) 1 − e^−0.1 = 0.095      1                  0.095
   3   4     red   (.9,.2,.2) 1 − e^−0.8 = 0.551      1 × 0.905 = 0.905  0.498
   4   8     red   (.9,.2,.2) 1 − e^−1.6 = 0.798      0.905 × 0.449 = 0.407   0.325
   5   1     blue  (.2,.3,.9) 1 − e^−0.2 = 0.181      0.407 × 0.202 = 0.082   0.015

   C = 0.095·gray + 0.498·red + 0.325·red + 0.015·blue = (0.791, 0.217, 0.226),   T(end) = 0.067
```

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-volume-render.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 240px;" alt="the transmittance stepping down from 1 to 0.07 along five samples with the per-sample weights as bars peaking at samples 3 and 4, and a red swatch of the resulting pixel color">

Note: Walk the table row by row; the point is that there is nothing in it a student cannot do. Air contributes nothing. The gray haze takes 9.5 percent. The two dense red samples take 82 percent between them and, in doing so, drive the transmittance from 0.9 to 0.08, so the blue sample behind them, which would have been a strong contributor on its own, gets a weight of 0.015: occluded, with no depth test anywhere, just the product. Six point seven percent of the ray reaches the background. The pixel is a red. Next: how the photographs train the field.

## Training: the march run backwards

Every operation on the last two slides is **differentiable**: the network, the exponentials, the products, the sum. So the whole march is a function from the weights θ to a pixel color, and

```text
   minimize over θ:   Σ over photos  Σ over pixels   ‖ C_θ(ray) − C_photo(ray) ‖²
```

- camera poses come from **structure from motion** (the capture era), so every photo pixel is a ray with a known answer
- nothing says where the surfaces are; the network **discovers** that density there makes many rays from many viewpoints agree with their photos
- the cost: **hundreds of network evaluations per ray**, millions of rays; hours to train, seconds per frame to render

Note: Training is the same march with the gradient flowing the other way. The photographs come with camera poses recovered beforehand by structure from motion, so each pixel of each photo defines a ray and the color it should produce. Gradient descent on the weights until rendered rays agree with the photographs; nothing tells the network where the surfaces are, it finds that putting density there is the only way to make rays from many viewpoints agree. The honest cost, hundreds of evaluations per ray, is why the next slides exist. Next: Gaussian splatting.

## 3D Gaussian splatting: back to primitives

**3D Gaussian Splatting** (Kerbl et al. 2023) keeps the "**learn it from photos**" idea but throws out the slow network. The scene is **millions of explicit primitives**, 3D **Gaussians**, little fuzzy translucent blobs:

```text
   each Gaussian:   position    μ        (where)
                    covariance  Σ (3×3)  (its 3D shape: an oriented, stretched ellipsoid)
                    color                (view-dependent)
                    opacity              (how solid)
```

- render by **projecting** ("splatting") each Gaussian to the screen and **blending** them **back-to-front**, a **rasterizer**, not a ray-marcher, so it runs in **real time**
- still trained by **differentiable rasterization**: gradient descent moves, stretches, recolors and fades the Gaussians until the render matches the **photos**, and **adds/removes** Gaussians as needed
- "back to primitives": the same **project-and-rasterize** pipeline you built, with a **fuzzy blob** as the primitive instead of a triangle

Note: Kerbl and colleagues in 2023 kept NeRF's winning idea, reconstruct the scene from photos by differentiable optimization, but replaced the slow implicit network with fast EXPLICIT primitives. The scene is millions of 3D Gaussians: each a small, soft, translucent ellipsoidal blob with a position, a covariance giving its shape and orientation, a view-dependent color, and an opacity. Rendering projects each onto the image plane, a small 2D footprint, and blends the footprints back to front: a rasterizer, the pipeline you built all quarter with a fuzzy Gaussian instead of a triangle, which is why it runs in real time. Training is still the inverse loop, and the optimizer also adds Gaussians where detail is missing and removes ones that went transparent. Next: what one splat looks like on the screen.

## A splat's footprint on the screen

A 3D Gaussian projected to the screen is a 2D Gaussian with covariance `Σ′ = J W Σ Wᵀ Jᵀ` (`W` the view rotation, `J` the Jacobian of your projection). Its footprint is an **ellipse**:

```text
   Σ′ = [ 0.5  0.3 ]      trace 0.7, det 0.01,  λ² − 0.7λ + 0.01 = 0
        [ 0.3  0.2 ]      λ₁ = 0.685, λ₂ = 0.015  →  radii √λ = 0.83 and 0.12 px,  tilt ½·atan(0.6/0.3) = 31.7°
```

<img src="../../textbook/figures/ls-splat.svg" class="media-shot" style="max-height: 200px;" alt="left: nested 1, 2, 3 sigma ellipses of the worked covariance with its principal axes drawn; right: three colored splats along one pixel row and their front-to-back composite">

- axes = eigenvectors of `Σ′`, radii = square roots of its eigenvalues: a **7-to-1 sliver**; the rasterizer touches only pixels within about three radii, and thin splats are how a Gaussian scene draws an **edge or a leaf**

Note: The one piece of linear algebra worth showing, because it is the course's own: the 3D covariance is rotated into view space and pushed through the Jacobian of the projection, exactly the V and P chain, and the result is a 2D covariance whose eigen-decomposition is the ellipse the rasterizer fills. The worked numbers: trace and determinant give the characteristic polynomial, the two eigenvalues give radii of 0.83 and 0.12 pixels, a seven-to-one sliver, tilted 31.7 degrees. Splats that thin are how the scene represents a sharp edge or a leaf. Next: blending three of them.

## Blending: the same formula, three samples deep

Along the pixel row of the figure, at `u = 0.45`, front to back:

```text
   splat        falloff at u      αᵢ = opacity·falloff    Tᵢ        wᵢ = Tᵢαᵢ    color
   1 red        e^−0.5  = 0.607   0.8 × 0.607 = 0.485     1         0.485        (.85,.20,.20)
   2 blue       e^−0.255 = 0.775  0.7 × 0.775 = 0.542     0.515     0.279        (.20,.45,.90)
   3 yellow     e^−7.03 = 0.001   0.9 × 0.001 = 0.001     0.236     0.000        (.95,.75,.15)

   C = 0.485·red + 0.279·blue + 0·yellow = (0.469, 0.223, 0.348);   T = 0.235 of the pixel shows the background
```

- `C = Σ Tᵢ αᵢ cᵢ`: the **NeRF march with three samples** instead of hundreds, sorted by depth once per frame
- the yellow splat is invisible here not because it is occluded (24 % of the ray reaches it) but because **its footprint does not extend to this pixel**; one row over it dominates

Note: The same front-to-back formula as the volume integral, with three splats standing in for hundreds of network samples; the splats are sorted by depth once per frame and every pixel runs this short sum. Read the rows: the red splat in front takes 48 percent, the blue behind it 28 percent of what is left, the yellow contributes nothing at this pixel because its own falloff is zero here, not because it is hidden; a quarter of the ray still reaches the background. Then the punchline of the two methods side by side: shade a point, accumulate along a ray, composite front to back, with a triangle, a network sample, or a Gaussian. Next: a real one, live.

## Learned scene, learned image

```text
   LEARNED SCENE (NeRF, 3DGS)              LEARNED IMAGE (diffusion)
   ────────────────────────────            ─────────────────────────
   one scene, from its own photos          all images, from an archive
   a camera you can move                   no camera, no scene
   renders new VIEWS of the same thing     samples NEW things
   a function of 3D position (a field)     a function of a noisy image (a denoiser)
   trained through a differentiable        trained by predicting noise;
   renderer (your V and P inside it)       renders nothing itself
   meet: score distillation (diffusion)    meet: ControlNet on your depth buffer
```

- 3D Gaussian splatting literally **rasterizes with a projection matrix**: it needs your **V** and **P**
- "shade a point, accumulate along a ray, composite front-to-back" is the same idea with a triangle, a network sample, or a Gaussian
- neither half erases the pipeline; both stand on it

Note: The contrast slide that organizes the era, now with both halves seen. Walk the two columns. A learned scene is one place, learned from its photographs, that you can re-photograph from anywhere; a learned image model is the whole archive, that you can sample from but never walk into. Both are trained by gradient descent through something: the scene through a renderer that contains your view and projection matrices, the image model through noise prediction. And they meet twice, in score distillation where the image model trains a scene, and in ControlNet where the pipeline's depth buffer steers the image model; the next topic takes both up. Close on the sentence that the pipeline is the substrate of both.
